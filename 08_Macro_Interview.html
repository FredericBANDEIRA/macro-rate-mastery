🎯 The 4-Pillar "Where Are Rates Going?" Pitch Framework

The single most ubiquitous question in global macro and rates sales & trading interviews is: "Where do you see interest rates going over the next 6 to 12 months, and how would you trade it?"

Junior candidates give vague macro opinions. Elite desk candidates structure their thesis across Four Institutional Pillars:

Pillar What You Evaluate Key Metrics & Catalysts
1. Macro Fundamentals (Cycle & Slack) Output gap, labor slack, growth momentum, and inflation composition. Core PCE vs. 2% target, SuperCore Services ex-housing, ECI wage growth, Sahm Rule threshold.
2. Central Bank Reaction Function Policy stance relative to neutral ($r^*$), Taylor rule prescription, and guidance credibility. Dot plot median vs. OIS market forwards, IORB/ON RRP plumbing, hawkish vs. dovish pause signals.
3. Supply & Demand Plumbing Sovereign fiscal issuance, auction indigestion, and foreign absorption capacity. Treasury QRA coupon vs. bill sizing, QT runoff caps, TIC foreign official flows, FX hedging costs for foreign buyers.
4. Trade Expression & Risk Asymmetry Choosing the exact duration-neutral or curve structure with positive carry or convex payoffs. 2s10s Steepener/Flattener, 2s5s10s Butterfly, TIPS Breakeven, SOFR options straddle, stop-loss and invalidation thesis.

📐 Master Macro & Rates Formula Compendium

Concept / Model Mathematical Formula Trading Meaning
Okun's Law $$\Delta u \approx -0.45(g - g^*)$$ A 1% growth rate above potential ($g^* \approx 2\%$) reduces unemployment by ~45 bps over one year.
Phillips Curve $$\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta(u_t - u^*) + \nu_t$$ Inflation depends on anchored expectations, labor slack relative to NAIRU ($u^*$), and supply shocks ($\nu$).
Taylor Rule (1993) $$i_t = r^* + \pi_t + 0.5(\pi_t - 2\%) + 0.5\tilde{Y}_t$$ Prescribes the nominal policy rate based on real neutral rate ($r^*$), inflation gap, and output gap.
Fisher Identity $$y_{\text{nom}} = r_{\text{real}} + \text{BEI} = r_{\text{real}} + \pi^e + \text{IRP}$$ Decomposes nominal Treasury yields into TIPS real yields, expected inflation, and inflation risk premium.
5y5y Forward Inflation $$F_{5,5} \approx 2 \times \text{BEI}_{10\text{Y}} - \text{BEI}_{5\text{Y}}$$ Isolates expected inflation between Year 6 and Year 10; pure gauge of central bank credibility.
DV01 Hedge Ratio $$\text{Notional}_1 = \text{Notional}_2 \times \frac{\text{DV01}_2}{\text{DV01}_1}$$ Sizing curve legs so total dollar price sensitivity to parallel shifts equals zero ($\text{DV01}_{\text{net}} = 0$).
2s5s10s Butterfly Spread $$\text{Fly} = -y_{2\text{Y}} + 2y_{5\text{Y}} - y_{10\text{Y}}$$ Measures curvature. Buying the fly (Long wings / Short belly) profits when the 5Y belly cheapens.
FX Hedged Yield $$y_{\text{hedged}} \approx y_{\text{foreign}} - (r_{\text{USD}} - r_{\text{domestic}}) + \text{CCBS}$$ Determines net return for foreign buyers (Japanese/European pensions) hedging dollar risk back home.
Twin Deficits Identity $$\text{CA} = (S_{\text{private}} - I) + (T - G)$$ A persistent fiscal deficit ($(T - G) < 0$) forces a Current Account deficit, requiring foreign bond inflows.
Risk Parity Weighting $$w_i \propto \frac{1}{\sigma_i} \implies \frac{w_B}{w_E} = \frac{\sigma_E}{\sigma_B}$$ Equalizing risk contribution across assets; requires leverage on low-volatility sovereign debt.

🏛️ Core Macro Framework & Central Banking Q&A

Q01
"NFP prints +100k above consensus, but the Unemployment Rate rises from 3.8% to 4.0%. How do rates react?"
▼

Desk Answer: You must distinguish between the Establishment Survey (payrolls) and the Household Survey (unemployment rate):

  • If unemployment rose because of an increase in the Labor Force Participation Rate (LFPR), the labor supply expanded, representing non-inflationary growth.
  • If participation was flat and actual household employment dropped, it signals underlying labor softening.
Initial algos buy USD and sell Treasuries on the headline beat. Within minutes, macro desks fade the move as the rising unemployment rate crosses key thresholds, driving 2Y yields lower.
Q02
"Calculate the Taylor Rule rate if neutral real rate r* = 1.0%, inflation = 3.5%, and output gap = +1.0%."
▼

Desk Answer: Using the classic Taylor (1993) rule:

$$i = r^* + \pi + 0.5(\pi - 2\%) + 0.5\tilde{Y} = 1.0 + 3.5 + 0.5(3.5 - 2.0) + 0.5(1.0) = 4.5 + 0.75 + 0.5 = 5.75\%$$
If the actual policy rate is 4.75%, the Fed is 100 bps behind the curve, signaling future hawkish repricing in front-end SOFR futures.
Q03
"Why does the Fed operate a floor system instead of a corridor system in an abundant reserves environment?"
▼

Desk Answer: With trillions in excess reserves created by QE, reserve supply intersects the demand curve on its completely flat, horizontal segment. Open market sales cannot alter overnight rates. The Fed must use administered rates:

  • IORB (Interest on Reserve Balances): The magnet rate paid to commercial banks.
  • ON RRP: The hard floor offered to non-banks (Money Market Funds) who lack IORB access.
EFFR trades comfortably between ON RRP (floor) and IORB (magnet), governed by GSE arbitrage.
Q04
"What is Quantitative Tightening (QT), and what signals that the Fed must halt balance sheet runoff?"
▼

Desk Answer: Under QT, the Fed allows a capped amount of maturing Treasuries and MBS to roll off without reinvestment. Initially, MMFs absorb issuance by pulling cash from the ON RRP facility (bank reserves unaffected). Once ON RRP reaches zero, bank reserves drain dollar-for-dollar.

The halt signal is the Lowest Comfortable Level of Reserves (LCLoR ~10-11% of GDP). When SOFR prints consistently above IORB and repo spreads spike (as in Sept 2019), the Fed must halt QT to prevent liquidity seizure.
Q05
"Why does the market OIS curve routinely price more cuts than the median FOMC Dot Plot?"
▼

Desk Answer: The Dot Plot reflects each member's single modal (baseline non-recession) forecast. Market prices reflect the probability-weighted mean across all distributions, including hard landings where rates drop 300 bps.

The divergence represents the market's priced probability of recession plus central bank credibility discounting.

📈 Yield Curve Trading & Inflation Derivatives Q&A

Q01
"Contrast a Bull Steepener with a Bear Flattener in terms of macro drivers and execution."
▼

Desk Answer:

  • Bull Steepener: Yields fall across the curve; front end plunges faster ($\Delta y_{2\text{Y}} \ll \Delta y_{10\text{Y}} < 0$). Driven by recession and Fed emergency rate cuts. Execution: Long 2Y / Short 10Y (DV01=0).
  • Bear Flattener: Yields rise across the curve; front end surges faster ($\Delta y_{2\text{Y}} \gg \Delta y_{10\text{Y}} > 0$). Driven by aggressive central bank tightening to fight inflation. Execution: Short 2Y / Long 10Y (DV01=0).
"Bulls cut rates, Bears hike rates." Steepeners expand spread; flatteners compress spread.
Q02
"Why is entering a steepener on an inverted yield curve an expensive negative carry trade?"
▼

Desk Answer: In an inverted curve, financing rates (repo/SOFR) are at the high front end. Shorting 10Y bonds forces you to pay high repo rates while receiving a lower coupon. Furthermore, rolling down an inverted curve causes bonds to shorten in maturity into higher yields, generating capital losses.

This "steepener bleed" eats into profits daily; traders must have high conviction on the timing of Fed cuts.
Q03
"What is the advantage of a regression-weighted butterfly over a standard 50/50 butterfly?"
▼

Desk Answer: A 50/50 fly allocates 50% DV01 to the 2Y and 10Y wings. Because the 2s5s slope moves with ~1.4x the volatility of the 5s10s slope, a 50/50 fly suffers from directional curve slope leakage. A regression fly weights wings by historical beta ($\beta$), making the position genuinely slope-neutral and isolating pure belly curvature.

Regression flies isolate whether the 5Y belly is rich or cheap without taking directional risk on curve slope.
Q04
"Decompose a 10Y nominal yield of 4.50% and 10Y TIPS yield of 2.00%. What is the breakeven rate?"
▼

Desk Answer: $\text{BEI} = y_{\text{nom}} - r_{\text{real}} = 4.50\% - 2.00\% = 2.50\%$. By the Fisher identity:

$$\text{BEI} = \mathbb{E}[\pi] + \text{Inflation Risk Premium} - \text{TIPS Liquidity Discount}$$
If actual cumulative headline CPI averages higher than 2.50% over the next decade, TIPS outperform nominals.
Q05
"What is the Cash-Swap Basis in the inflation market and why did it blow out to +60 bps in March 2020?"
▼

Desk Answer: $\text{Basis} = \text{ZCIS Swap Rate} - \text{Cash TIPS Breakeven}$. In March 2020, severe balance sheet constraints prevented primary dealers from absorbing funded cash TIPS sales. Cash breakevens plunged to 0.55% while unfunded swaps traded at 1.15%, blowing the basis out to +60 bps due to pure liquidity scarcity.

The dislocation resolved back to +15 bps once the Fed launched unlimited QE and SLR exemptions.

🌐 Cross-Asset Signals, Placements & Desk Brainteasers

Q01
"Why does the Copper/Gold ratio correlate strongly with 10Y US Treasury yields?"
▼

Desk Answer: Copper reflects cyclical industrial demand and global manufacturing output, while Gold is a safe-haven monetary asset that rallies on declining real yields and crisis fears. The Cu/Au ratio mirrors nominal GDP growth expectations, tracking 10Y yields with $R^2 \approx 0.70$.

Decoupling occurs during physical copper mine supply strikes or massive sovereign debt supply term premia spikes.
Q02
"Explain the Dollar Smile theory and where the US Dollar weakens across the smile."
▼

Desk Answer: The Dollar rallies at both extremes: Left Wing (severe global crisis/liquidity flight into USD cash) and Right Wing (US economic boom with higher Fed rates). The Dollar only weakens at the bottom of the smile, when global growth outside the US accelerates and capital leaves the US to chase higher EM returns.

Position short USD only during benign, synchronized global expansion.
Q03
"10Y UST yields 4.50%. US 3M rate is 5.25%, Japanese 3M rate is 0.25%. What is the net hedged yield in JPY?"
▼

Desk Answer: Forward hedge cost $\approx 5.25\% - 0.25\% = 5.00\%$. Net hedged yield $= 4.50\% - 5.00\% = \mathbf{-0.50\%}$.

Japanese institutions stop buying Treasuries because domestic JGBs (+0.80%) offer superior unhedged yield, removing an anchor on US long-end yields.
Q04
"Brainteaser: A bond has a modified duration of 8.0 and convexity of 80. If yields fall by 100 bps, what is the exact percentage price change?"
▼

Desk Answer: Using the Taylor series duration-convexity expansion:

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D \Delta y + \frac{1}{2} C (\Delta y)^2$$ $$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -8.0 \times (-0.01) + \frac{1}{2} \times 80 \times (-0.01)^2 = +0.08 + \frac{1}{2} \times 80 \times 0.0001 = +0.08 + 0.004 = \mathbf{+8.40\%}$$
Convexity added +40 bps of extra upside performance on top of the +8.00% duration gain.
Q05
"Brainteaser: If the 1-year spot rate is 5.0% and the 2-year spot rate is 4.0%, what is the 1-year forward rate 1 year from now (1y1y)? Is this an arbitrage?"
▼

Desk Answer: By no-arbitrage pricing: $(1 + s_2)^2 = (1 + s_1)(1 + f_{1,1})$

$$(1.04)^2 = (1.05)(1 + f_{1,1}) \implies 1.0816 = 1.05(1 + f_{1,1}) \implies 1 + f_{1,1} = \frac{1.0816}{1.05} \approx 1.0301 \implies f_{1,1} \approx \mathbf{3.01\%}$$
The market prices a 1-year rate of 3.01% in one year. It is not an arbitrage; it reflects expectations of aggressive rate cuts.
