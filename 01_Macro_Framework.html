Macro & Rates Hub Module 01

The Macro Framework

GDP accounting, the output gap, inflation dynamics, labor market anatomy, and the 4-quadrant business cycle clock for global macro rates trading.

🏛️ National Income Accounting & Potential Output

In global macro rates strategy, all pricing pivots around whether an economy is running above or below its non-inflationary capacity limit. We begin with the fundamental expenditure identity:

Aggregate Expenditure Identity
$$Y = C + I + G + (X - M)$$

Where $Y$ is Real GDP, $C$ is Household Consumption (~68% of US GDP), $I$ is Gross Private Domestic Investment (~17%), $G$ is Government Consumption & Investment (~17%), and $(X - M)$ is Net Exports (~-3%).

Potential GDP ($Y^*$) & The Output Gap

Potential GDP is the maximum sustainable output an economy can produce without generating excess inflation, governed by labor supply growth, capital accumulation, and Total Factor Productivity (TFP). The Output Gap measures the cyclical deviation:

Output Gap Formula
$$\text{Output Gap } (\tilde{Y}_t) = \frac{Y_t - Y_t^*}{Y_t^*} \times 100\%$$
💡
Rates Desk Transmission: A positive output gap ($\tilde{Y} > 0$) implies factor shortages, wage pressures, and capacity constraints $\rightarrow$ central banks hike policy rates $\rightarrow$ front-end yields surge, flattening or inverting the curve. A negative output gap requires accommodative policy $\rightarrow$ rate cuts, curve steepening.

Okun's Law: Linking GDP to Unemployment

Arthur Okun identified the empirical rule of thumb connecting changes in economic growth to shifts in the unemployment rate ($u$):

Okun's Law (Difference Form)
$$\Delta u_t \approx -\gamma \left( g_t - g^* \right) \quad \text{with } \gamma \approx 0.4 \text{ to } 0.5$$

If potential trend growth $g^*$ is $2.0\%$, an economy growing at $4.0\%$ ($+2.0\%$ above trend) typically experiences an unemployment decline of approximately $0.8\%$ to $1.0\%$ over the year.

The Expectations-Augmented Phillips Curve

The modern Phillips Curve dictates that inflation depends on inflation expectations, the labor market slack relative to NAIRU ($u - u^*$), and exogenous supply shocks ($\nu$):

Expectations-Augmented Phillips Curve
$$\pi_t = \pi_t^e - \beta (u_t - u^*) + \nu_t$$

Where $\pi_t^e$ is expected inflation (anchored by central bank credibility or market breakevens), $u^*$ is the Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment (NAIRU), $\beta$ is the slope parameter, and $\nu_t$ represents supply shocks (e.g. oil spikes, supply bottlenecks).

👥 Labor Market Anatomy: The Transmission to Wage Growth

Central banks pay meticulous attention to the labor market because services inflation cannot sustainably return to target if wage growth persistently exceeds labor productivity plus the inflation target:

Sustainable Wage Growth Condition
$$\text{Sustainable Wage Growth} \approx \text{Trend Productivity Growth} + \pi^* \approx 1.5\% + 2.0\% = 3.5\%$$

Trading Heuristic: The Beveridge Curve & Wage Spiral
The Beveridge curve maps Job Vacancy Rate ($V$) against Unemployment ($u$). When the curve is steep (high $V$, low $u$), cooling vacancies does not necessarily cause massive unemployment surges—allowing for the elusive "soft landing".

🧭 The 4-Quadrant Macro Regime Framework

Global macro portfolio managers and rates strategists map asset prices across the two principal dimensions of economic surprise: Growth (accelerating vs decelerating) and Inflation (rising vs falling).

1. Reflation / Recovery Growth ↑ | Inflation ↑

Environment: Output gap closing, earnings rebounding, monetary policy shifting from easy to neutral.

  • Rates Trade: Bear steepener, curve steepens then flattens, rising breakevens.
  • Equities: Cyclicals, Value, Small Caps, Financials outperform.
  • Commodities: Strong performance (Energy, Industrial Metals).
2. Overheating / Late Cycle Growth ↓ | Inflation ↑

Environment: Capacity constraints bite, central bank aggressively restrictive, credit spreads widen.

  • Rates Trade: Bear flattener $\rightarrow$ Curve inversion (2s10s negative), short duration.
  • Equities: Energy, defensive sectors, cash becomes attractive.
  • Commodities: Late-cycle commodity spike, precious metals resilient.
3. Contraction / Deflation Growth ↓ | Inflation ↓

Environment: Recession, layoffs, inventory destocking, central banks slash benchmark rates.

  • Rates Trade: Bull steepener! Long duration (Treasuries rally), front end cuts priced.
  • Equities: Broad declines, defensives (Utilities, Healthcare) hold up best.
  • Commodities: Collapse in cyclicals; High-grade sovereign debt reigns supreme.
4. Goldilocks / Early Expansion Growth ↑ | Inflation ↓

Environment: Productivity-driven expansion, benign pricing, monetary policy remains accommodative.

  • Rates Trade: Stable yields, range-bound rates, carry trades thrive.
  • Equities: Mega-cap Growth, Tech, discretionary rally aggressively.
  • Credit: High Yield spreads compress to cyclical tights.

🎯 Global Macro & Rates Trading Interview Masterclass

These questions test your ability to synthesize GDP, labor, inflation data, and real-time market pricing into actionable trading intuition. Click any question to reveal the institutional desk-level breakdown.

Q01
"NFP prints +120k above expectations, but the Unemployment Rate ticks up from 3.9% to 4.1%. How does the 2Y and 10Y Treasury yield respond, and why?"
▼

Desk Answer: You must distinguish between the Establishment Survey (payrolls) and the Household Survey (unemployment rate & labor force):

  • If the unemployment rate rose because of an increase in the Labor Force Participation Rate (LFPR)—i.e., hundreds of thousands of workers re-entered the labor force—the labor market is actually expanding its supply side, which is non-inflationary growth.
  • However, if LFPR was flat and actual household employment dropped, it signals divergence and rising slack.
Market Reaction: Typically, the algorithmic knee-jerk moves yields higher on the headline NFP beat. Within 3 minutes, macro desks scrutinize the unemployment rate tick-up and Average Hourly Earnings (AHE). If wage growth cooled and unemployment crossed key thresholds (e.g. Sahm Rule alert), front-end 2Y yields will reverse gains and drop (bullish rates), while the 10Y yield may rally as growth expectations moderate.
Q02
"What is the Taylor Rule, and how do rates traders use it to evaluate whether the Fed is behind the curve?"
▼

Desk Answer: John Taylor's 1993 rule prescribes the nominal policy rate ($i$) based on the equilibrium real rate ($r^*$), target inflation ($\pi^* = 2\%$), the inflation gap ($\pi - \pi^*$), and the output gap ($Y - Y^*$):

$$i_t = r^* + \pi_t + 0.5(\pi_t - \pi^*) + 0.5(Y_t - Y_t^*)$$

If $r^* = 1.0\%$, inflation is $3.5\%$, and output gap is $+1.0\%$:
$i = 1.0 + 3.5 + 0.5(3.5 - 2.0) + 0.5(1.0) = 4.5 + 0.75 + 0.5 = 5.75\%$.

Trading Implication: If the actual Fed Funds rate is at $4.75\%$ while the Taylor Rule dictates $5.75\%$, the Fed is $100\text{ bps}$ "behind the curve". Rates traders anticipate future hawkish surprises, pricing upward shifts in SOFR futures and front-end Treasury yields.
Q03
"Walk me through the mechanics of a 'Bear Flattener' vs a 'Bull Steepener' across the macro cycle."
▼

Desk Answer: The yield curve changes slope depending on whether rate moves are driven by policy hikes or cuts:

  • Bear Flattener (Late Cycle / Overheating): Yields rise across the board ("Bear"), but the short end (2Y) rises significantly faster than the long end (10Y/30Y). Why? The central bank is aggressively hiking rates to crush inflation. Long-term yields are constrained because investors expect the hikes to cause an eventual growth slowdown. Curve flattens or inverts (2s10s < 0).
  • Bull Steepener (Early Recession / Bust): Yields fall across the board ("Bull"), but the short end plunges far faster than the long end. Why? The central bank is slashing rates to inject liquidity. The long end falls less because of term premia and expectations of eventual economic recovery.
Cheat Sheet: "Bears hike, Bulls cut." Steepeners occur when the front end falls more (bull) or long end rises more (bear). Flatteners occur when front end rises more (bear) or long end falls more (bull).
Q04
"Explain the Sahm Rule and why macro desks watch it closely."
▼

Desk Answer: Formulated by former Fed economist Claudia Sahm, the rule states that a recession has historically begun when the 3-month moving average of the national unemployment rate ($U3$) rises by 0.50 percentage points or more relative to its minimum over the previous 12 months:

$$\text{Sahm Indicator} = \text{SMA}_3(u_t) - \min_{t-12 \le s \le t}(u_s) \ge 0.50\%$$

Unlike financial indicators (like yield curve inversions) which can lead recessions by 12–24 months with uncertain timing, the Sahm rule is a real-time coincident confirmation with zero historical false positives since 1970.

Market Signal: When the Sahm Rule triggers, rates desks immediately price emergency rate cuts, swap spreads widen, and 2s10s curves steepen aggressively as credit risk premia surge.
Q05
"Why do commodity price shocks present a dilemma for central bank reaction functions?"
▼

Desk Answer: A negative supply shock (e.g. oil embargo) acts as an external tax on consumers. It simultaneously pushes inflation up while depressing real disposable income and GDP growth (Stagflationary shock).

  • If the central bank hikes to suppress headline inflation, it exacerbates the economic contraction.
  • If the central bank cuts to support growth, it risks de-anchoring inflation expectations ($\pi^e$), leading to second-round wage-price spirals.
Desk Action: Central banks typically "look through" temporary commodity spikes provided long-term inflation expectations remain anchored. Traders watch 5y5y forward inflation swaps to see if credibility is slipping.
